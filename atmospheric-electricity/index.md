# Harvesting Electricity from the Atmosphere

The surface of the earth is slightly negatively charged (XXX charge density).
That's partly what makes it a good conductor, which lets us ground electrical
equipment for safety, use the ground as the return path for electrical power.
The upper atmosphere is positively charged, making it also a conductor (XXX
conductance). Between these lies the lower atmosphere, which is significantly
less conductive than the upper atmosphere (by roughly seven orders of
magnitude). The potential difference between the surface of the earth and the
ions 10 km above is around 500,000 volts. This is precisely the structure of an
electrical battery: two layers of ions of opposite charge, separated by a
dielectric. This battery is constantly charged by cosmic rays from above and the
decay of radioactive isotopes from below, making it a renewable resource.

In the 1920's, Plauson [XXX cite] investigated harvesting this energy with the
help of balloons. In his design, balloons float XXX m above the ground, tethered
by steel (XXX steel?) cables. The designs included intricate details like the
surface material of the balloons (spiked with  Radium plating to create even
more ionization), and the structure of the ground station (a layer of concrete
XXX meters thick,  a layer of glass XXX thick to insulate the tether from the
ground).

A hundred years later, our energy consumption has also increased by XXX.  But we
have more sophisticated versions of these technologies. Cables are lighter,
balloons can lift much heavily payloads and float higher, and we understand the
atmosphere much better, including where its ions are concentrated. One recent
patent goes so far as to dispense with balloons and cables altogether, replacing
them with a plasma conduit carved into air by ground lasers [XXX cite].

This document analyzes the economics of harvesting electricity from the
atmosphere using modern technology.  The conclusion is that given today's
technology, neither the power nor the energy of the electricity stored in the
atmosphere is enough to warrant an investment of this size. Here are the main issues:

* The total amount of energy stored in the atmosphere is ~55 MWh. A typical
datacenter would drain this energy within two hours. Cosmic rays would replenish
the energy gradually, but we're not sure how quickly. Regardless of this
recharge rate, the earth battery doesn't have enough capacity to feed a data
center.

* Assuming we use balloons to extract the energy, and assuming cosmic rays
replenish the charge as quickly as we withdraw it, the power production for a single
balloon would be around 10-50 MW. This is how much power a
solar farm that's 0.5 km x 0.5 km would produce during peak sunlight. 

* The cost of a single harvester would be around $30M. This puts it on par with
the construction cost of a solar farm that delivers a similar amount of power.
So there is no clear advantage there.
